# Vocabulary-first Hanzi explanations

Use this reference when a learner asks what one Hanzi means, especially when an isolated translation would be less memorable than the words that use it.

## Teaching aim

Help the learner acquire usable words first and let the character-level idea emerge from them. This reduces the tendency to over-analyse one character as though it must have one exact English equivalent.

This is a soft reference, not a mandatory sequence, word count, or a reason to avoid direct answers. Use a direct definition when it best serves the learner's immediate question. Otherwise, adapt the network to frequency, context, and the learner's current level.

## A compact word network

1. Start with one high-frequency or concrete anchor word.
2. Add several everyday collocations that reuse the character in distinct, memorable contexts.
3. If it adds value, add a second direction: a contrasting sense, a cultural or literary item, a domain-specific term, or a productive pattern. Label it honestly by frequency and register.
4. State a short character-level cue only after the learner has seen the words. Mark it as a memory cue, not a complete dictionary definition.
5. Reuse the new words in one short sentence or retrieval question.

Show every Chinese term with aligned tone-marked pinyin and an English or German gloss. Prioritise several useful terms over exhaustive coverage; do not pad the network with rare entries.

## Worked pattern: 烛

**Anchor and everyday network**

- **蜡烛** (*làzhú*) — wax candle.
- **烛火** (*zhúhuǒ*) — candle flame.
- **烛光** (*zhúguāng*) — candlelight.
- **烛台** (*zhútái*) — candlestick / candle holder.

**Second direction, labelled**

- **烛龙** (*Zhúlóng*) — Torch Dragon; a literary or mythological name, not ordinary daily vocabulary.

The everyday group lets the learner see candle, flame, light, and holder as one usable network. The cultural item shows that the imagery also appears outside routine conversation. Then give the compact cue: **烛** (*zhú*) commonly evokes a candle or torch in these words.

Avoid presenting the cue as the only possible translation or suggesting that **烛龙** is a common phrase for daily use.

## Worked pattern: 偿

If the learner asks whether **偿作业** (*cháng zuòyè*) works, do not begin and end with a single-word translation. Contrast a useful network:

- **偿还** (*chánghuán*) — repay, especially a debt.
- **赔偿** (*péicháng*) — compensate for a loss or damage.
- **补偿** (*bǔcháng*) — compensate or make up for something.
- **补作业** (*bǔ zuòyè*) — make up missed homework.
- **补交作业** (*bǔjiāo zuòyè*) — submit homework late.

The first three terms create a debt-or-loss network. The last two show the natural homework network. Only then make the practical contrast: **偿** (*cháng*) does not normally combine with **作业** (*zuòyè*); use **补作业** (*bǔ zuòyè*) or **补交作业** (*bǔjiāo zuòyè*) for the intended situation.

## Guardrails

- Keep the distinction between everyday words and literary, historical, proper-name, rare, regional, or technical vocabulary visible.
- Do not invent a word merely because its characters look compositional.
- Do not use etymology, radical analysis, or a one-character gloss as the main route unless the learner asks for it or it directly resolves confusion.
- Preserve ambiguity when the learner has not supplied enough context; offer the most likely word network and ask one focused follow-up only if the intended meaning would materially change the answer.
